Universal Representations for Robust AI-Generated Video Detection

Rhea Susarla1 and Maty Bohacek2

1 Fremont Union High School District    2 Stanford University

Methods for detecting AI-generated video fall into three long-standing categories: low-level analysis of generation artifacts, high-level tests of physical plausibility, and learned classifiers over deep embeddings. Detectors in each category generalize poorly beyond their training distribution, and, we argue, published comparisons between them are confounded by backbone size discrepancies. We present, to our knowledge, the first architecture that explicitly unifies all three categories of cues—density-based anomaly scores over frozen encoder features, fused with spectral and motion-coherence streams—and name it SEAM. Trained on a single generator from AIGVDBench, SEAM reaches 0.92 AUC on 19 unseen open-source generators, beating the state-of-the-art (SOTA) performance, and matching SOTA performance (0.87 AUC) on 11 unseen commercial generators, including Sora, Kling, and Pika. We further introduce SLOP, a dataset of 6,000 real and AI-generated videos from Instagram, TikTok, and X, and a taxonomy of such content. SEAM achieves the best in-domain performance on SLOP (0.96 AUC), and a SEAM trained on SLOP transfers to unseen commercial generators better than any baseline trained on the same data (0.89 AUC).

Key Takeaways
  • SEAM learns on real-only videos; clips are flagged from how far it scores from the real distribution instead of resembling a specific generator.
  • It is trained on a single generator, and generalizes to 30 unseen ones: SEAM has a 0.92 AUC on 19 unseen open-source generators and 0.87 on 11 unseen commercial ones.
  • The new social-media-based dataset, SLOP, covers videos online that people actually post (collected from Instagram, TikTok, and X, and sorted by style, format, and content).
  • The model tends to fail on more high production clips.

How can we build detectors that work on generators they haven't seen before?

SEAM fits a Gaussian density to features of real footage (only), and scores a clip by how far it is from that density. A video from a generator the model has never seen is detected for deviating from reality, essentially when it does not resemble anything it had been trained on.

Every method here is trained on a single generator (Open-Sora, 2,000 clips per class) and then tested on 19 unseen open-source and 11 unseen commercial generators. Published comparisons often put a small detector next to a much larger one, which makes it hard to tell a better method from a bigger backbone. So we retrained all nine baselines ourselves, on the same data, splits and frame extraction, with size-matched encoders of about 22M parameters.

SEAM leads on close-OOD and holds its own on far-OOD. Its close-OOD AUC is 0.923, against 0.885 for the next-best method. On commercial generators the methods are much closer, and differences of about 0.015 AUC are within the seed-to-seed noise we measured, so we read that column as a tie.

Social media videos are wildly different from current deepfake detection benchmarks

Generally, dataset benchmarks for deepfake detection are too clean to represent content often found on social media. Social media videos tend to be cropped, captioned, filtered, recompressed, etc. SLOP collects AI-generated and real videos from Instagram, TikTok and X so we can test on that kind of footage.

SLOP has 6,000 posts in a 50:50 split of real and AI-generated content, balanced across Instagram, TikTok and X. Splits are account-disjoint (70% train, 10% validation, 20% test), so no creator contributes to more than one split and a detector cannot exploit creator-specific cues. It mixes platform-representative random panels, dedicated AI-persona and AI-influencer accounts, and matched human creator accounts for the real class.

No existing dataset combines in-the-wild collection, platform-native processing, full-frame AI generation and AI-persona accounts with a balanced, split-ready release.

Re-fitting the densities and the small head recovers SLOP. SEAM trained on AIGVDBench scores only 0.569 on SLOP, and re-fitting the densities and head on SLOP's train split lifts that to 0.957 without touching the encoder. Across all five test sets SEAM has the best grid mean (0.769, against 0.742 for Effort and 0.717 for ForgeLens), and the best single transfer cell is 0.904, a MintVid-trained model evaluated on AIGVDBench. All three methods score low on VRT (0.49 to 0.63), whose real class is ASMR only.

Misclassifications made by SEAM

SEAM tends to miss more AI-generated content than infer real content to be AI. On SLOP, 72% of the errors are AI clips predicted as real, and the errors are spread roughly evenly across platforms (39% X, 30% Instagram, 31% TikTok). On AIGVDBench the errors concentrate in the unseen-generator conditions, with LTX and EasyAnimate the largest sources of missed fakes.

We then went through the SLOP clips that a SEAM trained on SLOP gets wrong, using the operating threshold selected for that model. Below are the errors whose videos we reviewed: 13 of 29 real clips flagged as AI and 20 of 73 AI clips passed as real, ordered from most to least confident. Each card shows the score and the split recorded for the clip.

Real flagged as AI. These include many clips from a single polished pet-influencer account, plus heavily edited footage: glitch and particle effects, template overlays. All of it is real; it just looks produced.

AI passed as real. Many are photoreal vertical talking-head or street-interview clips, several featuring the same recurring AI persona. Nothing about them looks like a lab-made fake.

Production style, not authenticity, seems to drive many errors. The model sometimes keys on how polished a video looks rather than on whether a camera captured it. We have not yet tested that explanation directly.
